I used to Google things mid-thought. Open a tab. Wait for results. Scan three links. Lose the thread entirely. Then try to remember what I was originally trying to figure out.

That was not learning. That was context switching disguised as research.

## What Changes at 1000 Tokens Per Second

Codex-Spark runs on Cerebras WSE-3. The numbers:

| Spec                      | Value                   |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Speed                     | 1000+ tokens per second |
| Context window            | 128K tokens             |
| Speedup vs standard Codex | ~15x                    |

At that speed, something fundamental shifts. The model does not make you wait. You ask. You get the answer. Before the question has fully left your working memory.

That changes the entire learning loop.

## The Old Loop

Ask question. Wait. Forget what you were thinking about. Get answer. Reconstruct context. Maybe follow up. Wait again. Repeat.

Each wait is a cognitive reset. You stop thinking forward and start thinking backward — trying to remember where you were.

Traditional classrooms have this problem built in. You raise your hand. You wait. The teacher finishes with someone else. By the time they reach you, the sharp edge of your question has gone dull.

Office hours are worse. You batch your questions, walk in, and half of them do not matter anymore because you figured some out or forgot the rest.

## The New Loop

Spark collapses the wait to zero.

Question. Answer. Deeper question. Answer. Deeper still.

This is what I mean by the learning treadmill. The model matches your thinking pace. It does not carry you — you still have to run. But it moves at exactly the speed you do. No waiting for it to catch up. No idle while it processes.

The treadmill metaphor is intentional. You set the pace. The machine matches it. Stop thinking and the treadmill stops with you.

## The Bottleneck Moves

This is the insight that surprised me.

When answers are instant, the constraint is no longer the answer. It is you. Specifically: how fast can you form the next meaningful question?

That is a completely different problem than "how do I get information."

It means the skill that matters is question quality. The ability to recognize what you do not know yet and articulate it precisely. Fast enough to keep the loop going.

Slow question formation breaks flow. Not because the model is slow. Because you are.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

I was learning a new cryptographic primitive last week. Zero-knowledge proofs. Dense material.

Old way: read documentation, get confused, open StackOverflow, read a Reddit thread, get slightly less confused, close the tab, start over.

New way with Spark: "Explain ZK proofs like I know elliptic curves but not proof systems." Answer arrived before I finished reading the question. "What's the difference between a prover and verifier in this context?" Answered. "Why does the verifier not need to see the witness?" Answered.

Six questions deep in under two minutes. Each answer built directly on the previous one. No reset. No reconstruction. Just forward momentum.

## The Loop Fundamentally Changes

Traditional learning is episodic. Bursts of effort interrupted by waiting.

Spark-enabled learning is continuous. One long thread of thinking, unbroken.

The difference is not just speed. It is structure. Episodic learning forces you to batch questions and accept context loss between sessions. Continuous learning lets you follow threads to their end.

You stop asking because you run out of questions, not because you run out of time waiting for answers.

## This Is Not Passive

The treadmill will stop if you do not keep running.

Spark is not a textbook you can skim. It is a conversation. It demands active thinking. You have to generate the next question. The model generates the next answer. Back and forth.

If you check out — if you stop generating questions — the loop dies. Nothing happens. The model waits for you.

That is actually a feature. The tool has no forward motion without your input. It cannot drag you somewhere. You have to walk.

## What I Got Wrong at First

I thought faster answers meant I could cover more ground.

I was right about that. But I mistook coverage for understanding.

I went wide. Touched twenty topics in an hour. Felt like I had learned a lot. Then tried to explain one of them and realized I could not.

Speed enables breadth. Depth still requires you to slow down and sit with something.

The learning treadmill can run at any speed. That does not mean you should always run it at max.

## The Real Shift

The bottleneck in learning has always been the question, not the answer.

Good teachers have always known this. Socrates did not have instant answers. He had the ability to ask questions that forced you to discover the answer yourself.

Spark inverts this. It supplies the answer instantly. Which means the only thing left is the question.

You have to become a better asker. More precise. More curious. More willing to say "I still do not understand this part" instead of nodding along.

The treadmill rewards honest inquiry. It does not reward passive consumption.

## What I Would Tell You

1. **Start with a specific question.** Not "explain machine learning." Something narrower: "Why does gradient descent not always find the global minimum?"

2. **Follow the thread.** Let the answer generate the next question. Go wherever it goes.

3. **Set a timer for 20 minutes.** Do not switch topics. Go deeper on one thing until you hit bedrock.

4. **Write what you learned.** Close Spark. Explain it in your own words. Not what the model said. What you now understand that you did not before.

5. **That gap is your actual learning.** The distance between what the model said and what you can explain — that is what you actually know.

## The Point

The treadmill moves at your pace.

The question is whether you have the stamina to keep asking.

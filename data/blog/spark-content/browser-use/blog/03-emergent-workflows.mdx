Something strange happens when you run a fast model in a browser harness long enough.

It stops doing what you told it to do. It starts doing what you meant.

Not a malfunction. The opposite.

## What Emergence Means Here

I am not talking about the model becoming sentient. I mean something narrower and more useful.

When inference is slow, the model executes instructions. One step, then the next.

When inference is fast, something different happens. The model stays ahead of the page loads. It has context from 5 steps back still active. It notices patterns across pages. It starts making decisions that were not in your prompt.

Those decisions are the emergence. Workflows that appeared from the combination of speed, browser context, and model intelligence. Nobody wrote them. They are good.

## A Real Example

I asked Spark to research a developer's GitHub profile and compile a brief.

The prompt said: collect repos, star counts, recent commit activity, primary languages.

What Spark actually produced: everything I asked for, plus a section on contribution patterns it inferred from the public graph, a note on the developer's apparent focus area based on repo naming conventions, and a flag on a repo that appeared dormant despite high star counts.

I did not ask for any of that. The model noticed those patterns because it was moving fast enough to hold multiple pages in context simultaneously.

That section on contribution patterns was the most useful part of the brief.

## Why Speed Enables This

A slow model doing multi-page research is context-limited by time. By the time it gets to page 5, page 1 has started to fade.

At 1000 TPS the model visits more pages before context pressure builds. Five pages still fit clearly in its attention window when it starts writing the summary.

The summary that emerges from 5 pages held clearly is qualitatively different. More connections. More inferences. More of what you actually wanted.

## When It Goes Wrong

Emergence is not always right.

Sometimes the model surfaces inferences that are incorrect. A pattern it thought it saw that was actually noise.

The discipline required: treat emergent outputs as hypotheses. Verify before acting.

The proportion of useful to incorrect emergent outputs in my testing: roughly 80/20. Four out of five were correct and useful. One was not.

That ratio is good enough to keep running. Just not good enough to automate downstream actions without review.

## The Design Implication

If you are building workflows with Spark and a browser harness, do not over-specify the prompt.

With a slow model that is correct. Ambiguity costs time.

With Spark the cost of ambiguity is low. And sometimes the space left by ambiguity is where the most useful emergent behavior lives.

Over-specifying kills emergence. Leave room. The model will fill it in ways you did not anticipate.

## What I Would Tell You

1. **Do not over-specify.** Specify the goal. Leave the path open.

2. **Expect emergence.** The model will synthesize beyond your prompt.

3. **Verify emergent outputs.** Treat them as hypotheses, not facts.

4. **The best outputs come from prompts that leave room.** Not from your most precise specifications.

The best results I have gotten from browser automation were not from precise prompts. They were from prompts that specified the goal but let the model figure out how to get there.

Speed made that safe. The model wanders fast, corrects fast, and occasionally produces something you could not have thought to ask for.

That is the workflow nobody programmed.
